01

What "independent" actually means

An independent support worker is a sole trader who provides NDIS supports directly to participants — without being employed by a registered provider. You invoice participants or their plan managers yourself, set your own hours, and take on the responsibilities (and freedoms) that come with running a small business.

This model has grown quickly in the NDIS because participants value continuity and personal choice in who supports them. For workers, it often means better pay rates, flexible scheduling, and genuine relationships with the people you support.

The trade-off: There is no employer to handle your tax, superannuation, insurance, or record keeping. You're responsible for all of it. This guide walks you through each piece.

02

Registered vs unregistered provider

The NDIS separates providers into two categories, and knowing which one applies to you shapes almost every other decision you make.

Registered providers are audited by the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission. They can work with all participants, including those whose plans are Agency-managed. Registration involves an application, a practice standards audit, and ongoing compliance obligations. It makes sense for larger organisations or workers delivering high-intensity supports (e.g. behaviour support, complex nursing).

Unregistered providers can work with participants whose plans are self-managed or plan-managed — which covers a large majority of participants. You do not need to be registered to work in this space. Most independent support workers start here, and many never need to register.

Around 40% of NDIS participants are plan-managed and a further 30% are self-managed. That's a large pool of people you can support as an unregistered sole trader.

If a participant is Agency-managed (their funding is administered directly by the NDIA), they can only use registered providers. Always confirm the participant's plan management type before agreeing to work together.

03

Set up your ABN

You need an Australian Business Number (ABN) to invoice participants or plan managers legally. Without one, you cannot operate as a sole trader in Australia.

Registration is free and takes about 15 minutes at abr.gov.au. You'll need your tax file number (TFN) and a description of your business activity — something like "NDIS personal support and community access services."

Once you have your ABN, you should also:

  • Register for GST if you expect to earn more than $75,000 per year (most independent workers won't hit this immediately, but it's worth planning for)
  • Open a dedicated business bank account — it makes tax time far simpler
  • Set aside 25–30% of every invoice for tax and superannuation (you are responsible for your own super as a sole trader)

Tip: Speak to an accountant familiar with NDIS sole traders early. The cost of an hour's advice pays itself back quickly in avoided mistakes.

04

Checks and clearances

Legally, an unregistered sole trader delivering NDIS supports only needs an ABN. That's the reality — and it's a gap in the system that surprises a lot of people. There are no checks or insurance requirements mandated by law for unregistered independent workers.

In practice, however, participants, plan managers, and most online platforms will expect you to have the following before they'll work with you:

  • NDIS Worker Screening Check — applied for through your state or territory screening unit. Costs vary by state (typically $11–$140). Processing can take 2–6 weeks, so apply early.
  • Working With Children Check (WWCC) — required by state law if you support participants under 18. State-specific, typically $80–$105.
  • National Police Check — routinely requested by plan managers and participants. Available through the ACIC website (~$42) or accredited agencies.
  • First Aid Certificate — HLTAID011 (Provide First Aid). Valid for 3 years; CPR component updated annually. Many participants ask for this, and it's genuinely useful in the field.

MyWattle strongly recommends independent workers hold all of the above, along with appropriate insurance and relevant training — not because the law requires it, but because your participants deserve to know the person supporting them has taken the role seriously.

05

Qualifications and training

No specific formal qualification is mandated for most personal support work under the NDIS. However, certain credentials significantly increase your employability and are expected by many participants:

  • Certificate III in Individual Support (CHC33021) — the industry standard entry-level qualification. Covers disability, aged care, and home and community care. Available through TAFEs and RTOs. Take 6–12 months part-time.
  • Certificate IV in Disability (CHC43121) — for workers who want to take on more complex supports or move toward team leader roles.
  • Medication Administration — required if you assist participants with medication. Check your state's requirements, as they differ.
  • Manual Handling — practical training in safe movement and transferring techniques. Often required by participants with high physical support needs.

Many community colleges and RTOs offer short online courses in areas like positive behaviour support, autism awareness, and mental health first aid. These add real value without the commitment of a full certificate.

06

Insurance you need

As a sole trader, your employer's insurance no longer covers you. You need to arrange your own policies.

  • Public Liability Insurance — covers you if a third party suffers injury or property damage as a result of your work. Aim for a minimum of $10 million cover. Typical annual cost: $300–$600 for support workers.
  • Professional Indemnity Insurance — protects you if a participant claims your advice or support caused them financial or personal harm. Some plan managers require this before agreeing to process your invoices.

Note: Some insurers offer combined PL + PI policies designed for sole-trader NDIS workers. Compare at least three quotes and confirm each policy explicitly covers NDIS disability support work.

Consider also whether you need income protection insurance. Unlike an employee, you have no sick leave — a month off the tools can mean a month without income.

07

NDIS pricing rates

The NDIS Pricing Arrangements and Price Limits document sets the maximum hourly rates that can be claimed from a participant's plan. You can charge less — you cannot charge more. The NDIS updates rates on 1 July each year, so always verify the current figures before invoicing.

How the pricing structure works:

  • Support category — most personal care and community access falls under Core Supports (Assistance with Daily Activities or Assistance with Social, Economic and Community Participation)
  • Time of day — weekday daytime, weekday evening (after 8pm), and overnight (sleepover) attract different rates
  • Day of week — Saturday, Sunday, and public holiday rates are progressively higher than the weekday rate

Get the current rates directly from the source: ndis.gov.au → providers → pricing arrangements. Download the current year's Pricing Arrangements and Price Limits PDF — it lists every support line item and its maximum rate.

As a sole trader you can negotiate any rate up to the price limit. Many independent workers charge the full rate — you have real overheads (insurance, admin, tax, super) that an employed worker's employer covers for them.

Use the NDIS support catalogue line item codes when invoicing — plan managers need the correct code to process your claim. MyWattle's invoice builder includes these codes so you don't have to look them up every time.

08

Finding your first participants

Most independent support workers build their client base through a combination of word of mouth, disability-specific platforms, and support coordinators.

  • Online platforms — Hireup, Mable, and Better Caring connect participants directly with independent workers. Create a strong profile: include your qualifications, clearances, and specific skills (e.g. autism experience, manual handling).
  • Support coordinators — Build relationships with local support coordinators. They are the connectors in the NDIS ecosystem and often have participants actively looking for reliable workers.
  • Local community networks — Disability advocacy groups, community centres, and carer networks are all worth engaging with. Participants find workers they trust through people they already know.
  • Social media — A simple LinkedIn profile or local Facebook community group can generate enquiries, especially in regional areas where platform-matched workers are scarcer.

When you do get an enquiry, meet the participant (and their support coordinator if they have one) before agreeing to anything. The fit between worker and participant matters enormously for both parties.

09

Record keeping requirements

Good records protect you and your participants. The NDIS requires that workers keep certain documentation, and participants (or their plan managers) can ask to see it.

  • Shift notes — a brief record of what was done each shift, any incidents, and how the participant was. These don't need to be essays — a few clear sentences per shift is enough.
  • Invoices — must include your ABN, the participant's name, the NDIS support line item (from the Price Guide), the date of service, hours worked, and the amount charged.
  • Service agreements — a written agreement between you and the participant (or their nominee) that sets out what supports will be delivered, when, at what rate, and cancellation terms.
  • Incident reports — if anything goes wrong during a shift (injury, property damage, a disclosure), it needs to be documented and reported as appropriate.
  • Tax records — keep all invoices, receipts for business expenses, and bank statements. The ATO requires you to keep these for five years.

Short-notice cancellations: The NDIS allows you to charge up to 100% of the agreed support if a participant cancels with fewer than 2 business days' notice. Make sure your service agreement includes your cancellation policy.

10

Running your business day-to-day

Once you're set up and working, the ongoing admin is manageable if you build habits from the start.

  • Clock on and off at every shift — this is your evidence if a participant or plan manager ever disputes hours worked.
  • Invoice promptly — plan managers typically pay within 5–10 business days of a valid invoice. The sooner you invoice, the sooner you're paid.
  • Stay current with your checks — NDIS Worker Screening clearances expire. First Aid certificates expire. Calendar reminders for renewals save you from scrambling.
  • Review rates annually — the NDIS releases updated pricing arrangements each financial year. Update your rates (and service agreements) when they change.
  • Communicate proactively — if something changes (your availability, a health issue, a policy update), tell your participants or their coordinator early. Reliability and honesty are what keep you working.
